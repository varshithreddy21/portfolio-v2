A company being cash-flow positive often matters more to me than it being PAT positive.
Of course, not every business has that luxury.
In manufacturing, chemicals, services, EPC, trading businesses, etc., credit is just part of how the industry works. Sometimes the entire supply chain runs on credit (both supplier and customer side). Sometimes you extend credit simply to keep relationships intact and sales flowing. It's unavoidable in most businesses we come across.
But then there are a few business models that work very differently.
Think airlines, D2C brands, restaurants, subscription businesses, insurance, gyms, or businesses that collect large customer advances upfront, like interior design or home automation companies.
These businesses get paid before they fully deliver. And I don't think people appreciate how powerful that can be.
Imagine two companies.
Company A reports healthy profits, but most of its money is stuck in receivables and inventory. On paper it looks great, but operating cash flow is negative.
Company B is still loss-making, but it collects advances from customers and enjoys supplier credit. Despite reporting losses, operating cash flow is positive.
Personally, I would pick Company B almost every time.
I am perfectly comfortable with a business reporting accounting losses, as long as it's cash flow positive — because at the end of the day, being cash flow positive (or working capital negative) beats being PAT positive with negative cash flow. 
(There are obviously other factors to consider, and this is just one factor of all)

- On to Cash Runway Calculations:
Continuing the above - It also changes how I think about cash runway.
Let's say a company has:
•	EBITDA burn: ₹80L/month
•	Interest + principal payments: ₹20L/month
•	Cash available: ₹5 Cr
Most people would say: Runway = ₹5 Cr ÷ ₹1 Cr = 5 months
Fair enough…!!
Honestly, I look at runway a little differently.
Runway only gives you a sense of where a company stands at a point in time—it's not a countdown to when the company dies. There are several moving parts that can change it, and working capital is one of the biggest.
A favourable working capital cycle can significantly extend a company's runway (like in the example below), while a working-capital-intensive business can see its runway shrink much faster.
That's why I rarely look at runway in isolation.
Now let's say this company manufactures furniture. And It collects 40–50% advances from customers, takes about 3–4 months to complete the order, and also gets 30 days of supplier credit.
That changes everything.
Customer cash starts coming in before the company has even spent much on that order.
(Yes, a portion of those advances eventually gets used to buy raw materials and hold inventory)
But if fresh orders keep coming in, those advances continue funding a meaningful part of working capital.
So if the company is "burning" ₹1 Cr every month at EBITDA level, but is also collecting roughly ₹50L of customer advances every month, is it really burning ₹1 Cr? Not really.
The effective cash burn is closer to ₹50L/month.
Which means:
•	EBITDA burn: ₹80L
•	Interest + principal: ₹20L
•	Customer advances: ₹50L/month
•	Net cash burn: ₹50L/month
So instead of a 5-month runway, the business actually has closer to 10 months.
Of course, this assumes sales remain steady and customer advances continue coming in (Which is why regular portfolio monitoring is very important as an investor).
The moment order inflows slow down, advances reduce, working capital changes—and your runway changes with it.
That's why I rarely look at runway as a fixed number. To me, it's a metric based on today's assumptions.
Working capital is often one of the biggest variables behind that number. Sometimes it extends your runway significantly. Sometimes it shortens it.
So the next time you look at a company that's still reporting losses, don't stop at the P&L.
Sometimes, the safest borrower isn't the profitable one—it's the one whose customers are funding the business.
