Every deal that crosses my desk gets put through the same rigorous evaluation. Here's what goes through my mind when I'm assessing an opportunity.

## The Three Pillars

Every assessment boils down to three questions:

1. **Can they deliver?** (Capacity)
2. **Will they deliver?** (Willingness)
3. **What if they don't?** (Recovery)

### Capacity: The Numbers

This is where the quantitative analysis comes in. I'm looking at:

- **Track record** — Historical performance and consistency
- **Growth trajectory** — Is the business growing fast enough to meet obligations?
- **Margins** — Higher margins mean more flexibility
- **Cash flow** — How quickly does the operation convert activity into cash?
- **Existing obligations** — What's already committed on the balance sheet?

### Willingness: The Track Record

Numbers tell half the story. The other half is about the people:

- **Leadership track record** — Have they navigated challenges before?
- **Payment history** — Any defaults or delayed commitments?
- **Transparency** — How open is management about challenges? The people who flag problems early are the ones you want to work with
- **Skin in the game** — How much have the founders retained? Alignment matters

### Recovery: The Downside

I never evaluate a deal expecting it to go bad. But I always evaluate knowing it might. What can be recovered if things don't go as planned?

## Red Flags That Kill Deals

Regardless of how good the numbers look:

1. **Aggressive reporting** — Inflating numbers is a serious concern
2. **Revolving obligations** — Using new commitments to pay off old ones is unsustainable
3. **Unexplained gaps** — If I can't trace where things went, I can't assess the risk
4. **High concentration** — Over-reliance on a single client or revenue source
5. **Misaligned priorities** — If personal spending is mixed with business expenses

## The Assessment Document

Every deal gets a thorough write-up covering:

- **Executive summary** — What the deal is and why we're doing it
- **Company overview** — Business model, market, competitive position
- **Financial analysis** — Historicals, projections, and key metrics
- **Risk assessment** — What can go wrong, and how likely is it?
- **Structuring recommendations** — Terms, conditions, and safeguards
- **Recommendation** — Proceed, decline, or proceed with conditions

## Conclusion

Evaluation is equal parts art and science. The science is in the analysis — the ratios, the models, the stress tests. The art is in reading between the lines — understanding the vision, sensing the dynamics, and making a judgment call when the data is incomplete.

The best evaluators aren't the ones who never lose. They're the ones who understand which risks are worth taking.
