## Where Providing Credit Is Unavoidable

In manufacturing, chemicals, services, EPC, and trading businesses, credit is just part of how the industry works:

- Sometimes the entire supply chain runs on credit — both on the supplier and the customer side
- Sometimes you extend credit simply to keep relationships intact and sales flowing

It's unavoidable in most businesses we come across.

## The Businesses That Work Differently

But then there are a few business models that behave very differently — think:

- Airlines
- D2C brands
- Restaurants
- Subscription businesses
- Insurance
- Gyms
- Businesses that collect large customer advances upfront, like interior design or home automation companies

These businesses get paid before they fully deliver. And I don't think people appreciate how powerful that can be.

## Company A vs. Company B

Imagine two companies.

**Company A** reports healthy profits, but most of its money is stuck in receivables and inventory. On paper it looks great, but operating cash flow is negative.

**Company B** is still loss-making, but it collects advances from customers and enjoys supplier credit. Despite reporting losses, operating cash flow is positive.

Personally, I would pick Company B almost every time.

I am perfectly comfortable with a business reporting accounting losses, as long as it's cash-flow positive — because at the end of the day, being cash-flow positive (or working-capital negative) beats being PAT positive with negative cash flow.

*(There are obviously other factors to consider — this is just one factor among many.)*

## On to Cash Runway Calculations

This changes how I think about cash runway too.

Let's say a company has:

- **EBITDA burn:** ₹80L/month
- **Interest + principal payments:** ₹20L/month
- **Cash available:** ₹5 Cr

Most people would say:

> Runway = ₹5 Cr ÷ ₹1 Cr = 5 months

Fair enough.

Honestly, I look at runway a little differently. Runway only gives you a sense of where a company stands at a point in time — it's not a countdown to when the company dies. There are several moving parts that can change it, and working capital is one of the biggest.

A favourable working capital cycle can significantly extend a company's runway (as in the example below), while a working-capital-intensive business can see its runway shrink much faster. That's why I rarely look at runway in isolation.

### A Worked Example: Furniture Manufacturing

Now let's say this company manufactures furniture, and it:

- Collects 40–50% advances from customers
- Takes about 3–4 months to complete an order
- Gets 30 days of supplier credit

That changes everything. Customer cash starts coming in before the company has even spent much on that order.

*(Yes, a portion of those advances eventually gets used to buy raw materials and hold inventory.)*

But if fresh orders keep coming in, those advances continue funding a meaningful part of working capital.

So if the company is "burning" ₹1 Cr every month at the EBITDA level, but is also collecting roughly ₹50L of customer advances every month — is it really burning ₹1 Cr? Not really. The effective cash burn is closer to ₹50L/month.

Which means:

- **EBITDA burn:** ₹80L
- **Interest + principal:** ₹20L
- **Customer advances:** ₹50L/month
- **Net cash burn:** ₹50L/month

So instead of a 5-month runway, the business actually has closer to **10 months**.

Of course, this assumes sales remain steady and customer advances continue coming in — which is why regular portfolio monitoring is very important as an investor. The moment order inflows slow down, advances reduce, working capital changes — and your runway changes with it.

## Conclusion

That's why I rarely look at runway as a fixed number. To me, it's a metric based on today's assumptions. Working capital is often one of the biggest variables behind that number — sometimes it extends your runway significantly, sometimes it shortens it.

So the next time you look at a company that's still reporting losses, don't stop at the P&L.

Sometimes, the safest borrower isn't the profitable one — it's the one whose working capital cycle is sorted.
